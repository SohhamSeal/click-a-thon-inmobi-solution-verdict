Chapter 1: The Problem Verdict Solves

Why "find the segment that moved the most" is not good enough.

Simple first

The business story

Imagine you run a mobile ad network like InMobi. Millions of ad requests flow every hour. For each request you track: did we fill it? Did the user see an ad? Did they click? How much revenue did we earn?

Operations teams watch metrics like fill rate (what fraction of requests got an ad), CTR (click rate), and eCPM (revenue per thousand impressions). When one of these drops suddenly, someone needs to answer: which slice of traffic broke, and why?

That is the Automated Root Cause Analyst problem from the ClickHouse Click a thon 2026. Verdict is the solution built for that track.

Analogy: Your city's average temperature dropped 5°C. The naive fix is to find which neighborhood cooled the most and blame it. But maybe one big factory shut down in Industrial District, and every neighborhood that has factory workers also looks cold. Those neighborhoods are passengers, not the cause.

The obvious approach (and why it fails)

The obvious approach:

  1. Break traffic into segments (region, OS version, device model, etc.)
  2. Rank segments by how much each metric moved
  3. Report the worst one

This fails in three ways that actually appear in the hackathon data.

Failure 1: Passengers look like drivers

Real incident: Android 15 fill rate collapses.

Every phone model that mostly runs Android 15 also shows a big drop. Galaxy S23 looks guilty. But Galaxy S23 did not break. Android 15 broke, and Galaxy S23 is just along for the ride.

Ranking cannot tell them apart. Both look identical from the top.

Failure 2: Interactions hide in plain sight

Some incidents only exist at the intersection of two dimensions.

Example shape: only APAC × iOS 18.1 is wrong. Look at APAC alone: mildly off. Look at iOS 18.1 alone: mildly off. Neither crosses a threshold. The real problem is invisible to any one dimensional scan.

Failure 3: Compensating pairs cancel out

One ad format's eCPM falls 30%. Another rises 25%. The total eCPM barely moves. A detector watching only the aggregate sees a healthy system.

Measured on this corpus: total eCPM moved only 0.11% while the two legs moved −30% and +25%.

Going deeper

What Verdict does differently

Verdict replaces ranking with proof and publishes what it cleared.

Naive approachVerdict approach
Rank by movement sizeExplain away test: remove candidate, does parent return to normal?
One detector on totalsTwo detectors: temporal (vs history) + structural (vs siblings)
Silent about what was skippedCoverage ledger: every untested cell with a reason
LLM decides the answerStats decide; LLM only writes prose from fixed numbers

The five stage pipeline

When you run verdict investigate --start 2026-07-05 --hours 24:

  1. Detect — scan the lattice. Did any segment move vs its baseline or vs its siblings?
  2. Correct — apply Benjamini Hochberg so thousands of tests do not flood false alarms.
  3. Localize — for each incident, run counterfactuals: sufficiency, minimality, maximality, holdout.
  4. Score — compute confidence from five weighted components.
  5. Narrate & publish — LLM writes summary (optional), everything goes to ClickHouse + trace.

What a good answer looks like

A strong Verdict case says:

  • What moved: fill rate fell 3.4% globally
  • Who is accused: os_version = Android 15
  • Why we believe it: removing Android 15 restores global fill rate (sufficiency passed)
  • Why alternatives were cleared: Galaxy S23 failed sufficiency (passenger)
  • Confidence breakdown: each component with its weight and score
  • Full trace: every step, SQL query, and timing

Rate vs traffic mix (another trap)

Fill rate can fall for two completely different reasons:

  • Rate problem: a segment started failing to fill (real incident)
  • Mix problem: more traffic went to a segment that was always worse at filling (not a bug)

Verdict decomposes the movement into rate, mix, and interaction terms so a demand shift is not reported as an outage. On the Android 15 incident, essentially all of the −3.4% move is within segment rate, not mix. That supports "real outage" rather than "traffic shifted."

Known honest miss: iOS 17.5

On the unseen release bundle (July 6 to 10), the true cause of fill rate collapse was os_version = iOS 17.5 (40% relative drop, easy to verify in SQL). Verdict reported publisher_tier = tier_3 instead for fill rate.

Why: dimension attributes were regenerated across the boundary. Historical baselines for segment level rates became meaningless even though volume baselines stayed fine. This is documented as a known weakness, not hidden. See Chapter 5 and 7 for details.
